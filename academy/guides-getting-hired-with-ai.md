## It's a Tough Market

It's not an easy market. A few years ago it was a lot easier. AI makes some things harder for us, but it also makes it easier to build things, and you can use that.

I recommend five steps, and you keep repeating them:

1. Network as much as possible.
2. Apply, through your network and online.
3. Build projects.
4. Interview.
5. Look back at what worked and what didn't.

There's no shortcut, because you need time for each step.

## Applications Get Lost in the Queue

I've seen job postings that went up two hours ago and already have 200 applications. Some people now tell an agent to watch for new AI engineering jobs and apply as soon as one appears. So every posting gets a huge number of applications, and yours is one of them.

With a referral, you skip that queue. If someone inside the company refers you, you're at the front, not number 201. That's why you need to network more now than you did before.

## Reach Out With a Question, Not Your CV

If you write to someone on LinkedIn "here's my CV, can you refer me", they'll probably not answer, and I wouldn't either. You won't get far by sending as many connection requests as possible without making them personal.

Be curious about the other person instead:

- Read something they wrote, for example a post on their company's engineering blog, and ask them a question about it.
- If you built something similar, say so and show your project.
- Only then ask whether their company is looking for people.

When I was a hiring manager, people who asked me a few questions on LinkedIn and then invited me for lunch always got a meeting. I had no reason to say no to meeting an interesting person. It didn't always end in a referral, but I met interesting people that way.

## Go to Meetups and Talk to Speakers

Meetups work better than cold messages. Speakers are good people to talk to, because after their talk you already have something to ask. Go up to them afterwards, ask about the talk, and tell them if you've worked on something similar.

You won't get a referral from every conversation, and you don't have to. Don't try to turn people into referrals right away. Get to know the person instead. Give it time, because the person you meet now may move to another company in three years and remember you.

I'm an introvert, and these events exhaust me, but over the years I trained myself to talk to people. Once I'd practiced enough, I stopped feeling terrible about talking to strangers.

## Build Projects for the Domain You Want to Work In

Your portfolio should show that you have AI experience and that you understand the domain of the companies you apply to.

Here's how to find the right projects:

1. Pick the kind of company you want to work for. Say you want to work at OLX, an online marketplace where I used to work.
2. List similar companies, like eBay or marketplaces for used clothes.
3. Read their job descriptions to see which problems they solve.
4. Read their engineering blogs. Most of these companies have one.
5. Build projects that solve those problems. Use coding agents like Claude Code, and use the skills you want to pick up.

Then, when you get an interview, you have a lot to talk about. You researched the domain and built something in it. The interviewer might say "we did something similar, how did you solve this?", and you can tell them.

## Use the Experience You Already Have

You don't need a junior position. Position yourself as a mid-level specialist and build on what you already know. If you worked in insurance, target insurance companies that are hiring AI engineers. You bring the domain, you learn the fundamentals, and you build many projects in that domain.

Get a subscription to Claude or Codex. You can start with the cheapest plan, but in my experience it isn't enough. A second low-cost plan, like OpenCode Go, helps a lot, and then you can see whether you need more.

## Fundamentals First, Then Projects

On social media there's a new framework every day. It can feel like the list of skills employers want grows faster than you can learn. Tools come and go, but you keep using the same fundamentals. RAG works the same way it did three years ago, and you always have to evaluate your models.

Learn the fundamentals first, then apply them right away in projects. If you keep doing that, you have less and less to learn before you're useful at work.

## Learn From Every Interview

After each interview, write down every question you were asked. If you feel you could have answered one better, note it, or write a blog post about it. When you hear it again, it's easier to answer. Your first interview will probably not be your best one, but by the 10th you'll feel a lot better.
